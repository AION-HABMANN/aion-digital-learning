# Trainer guide

## How to use this document

- **Before the day:** read the lesson plan above and the model solution of the case study (UE5–6). Decide which Optional cards and blocks your group will skip.
- **During the day:** use the section of the unit you are in (UE4 coaching, UE6 debrief, UE7 feedback round, UE10 debrief). Show a model solution only after the participants have worked.
- **Reference answers:** they use the facts and the numbers printed in the Task document. Figures that the plan does not print are Case assumptions. Effort is rated by the printed cost rule taught in Materi A5; user impact and risk are judgements.
- **What is not here:** the Materi (the study material) and the Task (the worksheet) are separate documents and are handed out. This one is not.

## Assessment criteria (Bewertungskriterien)

Use these criteria to read what participants hand in and to give feedback in words. They are the same on every day; the reference answers below say what meets them on this day.

| Criterion | Level | Meets | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The user's side** | 1 | Describes and sorts from the learner's side; every statement points at a printed fact. | Mostly from the learner's side; some statements are about the design only, or no fact is named. | Describes the platform, not the learner; no fact named. |
| **Weighing under limits** | 2 | Rates user impact, effort and risk with a reason for each; effort follows the printed cost rule; budget and time are checked, or the overrun is explained. | Ratings given, reasons thin, or effort does not follow the rule. | Ratings missing or without reasons; the limits are ignored. |
| **Prioritising** | 2 | Gives a reason for position one (reach, cost or what the others depend on); the order fits the ratings. | An order is given; the reason covers the whole set or is generic. | No order, or no reason. |
| **Missing information** | 2 | Names something specific that would change the decision, and how to find it out. | Names a gap but not how to find it out. | “More data” with nothing specific. |
| **Conflict of goals** | 3 | Names the day's conflicting goals, says who or what loses, and justifies the order. | Names the conflict; the loser is not named. | Presents a plan in which everything wins. |
| **Decision under uncertainty** | 3 | Decides in one sentence, names an unknown, gives a reversal figure and a time, and says what is given up. | Decides; the reversal or the give-up is missing or vague. | Does not decide, or gives up nothing. |

**How to give feedback.** Say what is strong, name one criterion that is only partly met, and give one concrete next step. A choice that differs from the reference is not an error if the reason is clear and refers to a printed fact.

# UE2–3 · Level 1 tasks

## Block 1.1 · model answers (UE2)

*Block 1.1: Eight problems: measuring, reading or hypotheses, and a symptom taken for a cause · Core · about 13 min.*

### Reference: Eight problems into Measuring, Reading, Hypotheses

**Reference answer.** Measuring: facts 1, 2, 7, 8 · Reading: facts 3, 4 · Hypotheses: facts 5, 6

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| 1 · 31 metrics, none marked as the one that matters | Measuring. Too many numbers and no priority: a measuring problem. |
| 2 · “Engagement” is clicks, with no definition | Measuring. A KPI with no definition: a measuring problem. |
| 3 · “70% drop-out, so the content is too hard” | Reading. A symptom taken for a cause: a reading problem. |
| 4 · A rise called a success; last month had a holiday | Reading. Two periods that are not comparable: a reading problem. |
| 5 · Five measures in one month, no way to tell them apart | Hypotheses. Changes cannot be told apart: a hypothesis problem. |
| 6 · No measure states an expected effect | Hypotheses. No expected effect, so nothing to test: a hypothesis problem. |
| 7 · Every click stored with a name; the privacy notice is silent | Measuring. Data collected beyond need and without notice: a measuring problem. |
| 8 · “Time spent” counts a tab left open overnight | Measuring. A metric that does not measure what its name says: a measuring problem. |

*Trainer note.* The sort is objective in its core: 31 metrics, a KPI with no definition, tracking with a name and no notice, and time spent that counts an open tab are measuring; a symptom taken for a cause and a rise compared with a holiday month are reading; five measures at once and no expected effect are hypotheses. Fact 5 could also be argued as reading (the effect cannot be read); the test is whether the problem is in how the change was set up. A learner who argues another placement with a reason defends the choice. Plan: Arbeitsauftrag 1, causes, suspected problems, measures.

### Reference: The symptom taken for a cause

**Reference answer.** 3 · “70% drop-out, so the content is too hard”

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · 31 metrics on the dashboard | No | Not a jump: too many numbers, a measuring problem. |
| 2 · “Engagement” is clicks | No | Not a jump: an undefined metric, a measuring problem. |
| 3 · “70% drop-out, so the content is too hard” | Yes | Reference choice: a number (the symptom) is turned into an explanation (the cause) with no check. |
| 4 · A rise called a success, vs a holiday month | No | Defends: a wrong comparison; the conclusion is drawn from a number that does not mean what is claimed. |
| 5 · Five measures in one month | No | Not a jump: a set-up problem. |
| 6 · No measure states an expected effect | No | Not a jump: missing hypotheses. |
| 7 · Every click stored with a name | No | Not a jump: a data protection problem. |
| 8 · “Time spent” counts an open tab | No | Not a jump: a metric that does not measure what it says. |

*Trainer note.* Element 3 is the reference: it turns the symptom (70% leave) into a cause (the content is too hard) with no check. Element 4 also defends, as a misreading from a wrong comparison. Look at the reason: it should name other possible causes. The plan's goal is to develop an intuitive understanding of data.

:::note 1.1 · What else could cause a 70% drop-out rate with short time spent?
**Model answer (an example).** “Too hard” is one guess among many: a long sign-up form, a slow page, a mismatch between the course title and its content, or a mobile layout could all make learners leave early, and short time spent fits all of them. The report looked at none of these, so it took a symptom for a cause.

**Why.** Data shows symptoms, not causes. A good answer names several possible causes and says that the first explanation was never checked (Materi A3).

**Look for.**
- Names at least two other causes.
- Says why “too hard” was a jump.
:::

:::note 1.1 · Which problems do you suspect, and which measures do you suggest?
**Model answer (an example).** Suspected: reading (facts 3 and 4), hypotheses (5 and 6) and measuring (1, 2, 7, 8). 1. Define three KPIs with a written definition and pick one to watch (facts 1 and 2). 2. Write a hypothesis for each change and launch one at a time (facts 5 and 6). 3. Watch five learners on the screens where they leave, before naming a cause (fact 3). 4. Stop storing names with clicks and update the privacy notice (fact 7).

**Why.** The plan asks which problems you suspect and which measures you suggest. A good answer names the kind of problem and ties each measure to a fact (Materi A2, A3).

**Look for.**
- Names the kinds of problem.
- Names the fact each measure answers.
- Starts each measure with a verb.
:::

### Reference: Three options for a KPI-based decision

**Reference answer.** Reference order: A (Improve the UX (navigation and structure)), then B (Expand the content), then C (Increase the marketing)

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A · Improve the UX (navigation and structure) (€13,000, 4 weeks) | Yes | It goes to a likely cause of the drop-out; moderate cost, and it can be tested. Reference ratings: KPI impact High, effort Mid (by the printed cost), risk Mid; position 1. |
| B · Expand the content (€24,000, 7 weeks) | Yes | More content does not address why learners leave; high effort. Reference ratings: KPI impact Low, effort High (by the printed cost), risk Mid; position 2. |
| C · Increase the marketing (€20,000, 5 weeks) | Yes | More learners arrive into the same drop-out; it raises the number of leavers, not the completion rate. Reference ratings: KPI impact Low, effort High (by the printed cost), risk High; position 3. |

*Trainer note.* Data-based prioritisation: ask which KPI each option would move.

:::note 1.2 · Justify your decision: develop a data-based prioritisation
**Model answer (an example).** I put A first: the drop-out is the problem, and the structure and navigation are likely causes that can be tested, at €13,000 and four weeks. B comes second: more content does not touch why learners leave, but it does little harm. C comes last: more marketing sends more learners into the same drop-out, so the KPI I care about does not improve and the cost is high.

**Why.** The plan's goal is to develop a data-based prioritisation. A different first choice defends if the reason names the KPI it would move and the limited budget.

**Look for.**
- Says what each option does to the KPI.
- Names the limited budget or the time pressure.
:::

## Feedback with a Strategic Perspective (UE7)

## Transition from Level 2 to Level 3 (Übergang)

Level 2 asks a participant to decide among measures. Level 3 asks them to decide as a manager, between goals that pull apart. The day's conflict is: **Conflicting goals: data against intuition, more data against more insight** (Materi B2).

**A participant is ready for Level 3 (Route 2, Task 2) when:**

1. the Core of Task 1 is done and the reason for position one names a reach, a cost or a dependency;
2. the missing information is specific and comes with a way to find it out;
3. they can say the day's conflict in one sentence and name what loses when each side wins.

**If a participant is not there yet:**

- Ask for the reason of position one again, using the “How to decide” box of Materi A5.
- Pair them for UE8 with someone who is ready, for the brief and the first block.
- Give them the “In plain words” box of Materi B1 before the brief; Route 2 does not need Route 1 answers.

Route 2 does not require Route 1: a participant may start the transfer project without having finished Task 1, and use their own answers in the memo if they have them.

## Block 3.1 · model answers (UE9)

*Block 3.1: The strategy, the tracking, the hypotheses and the decision architecture · Core · about 10 min.*

### Reference: The data-driven strategy

**Reference answer.** Hypothesis first: three KPIs, written hypotheses, one change at a time, and tracking only what the hypotheses need

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis first: three KPIs, written hypotheses, one change at a time, and tracking only what the hypotheses need | Yes | Reference strategy: turns data into insight and insight into a decision, and respects privacy. |
| Track everything and look for patterns | No | Weak: more data without questions repeats the misreading, and tracking everything is a privacy risk. |
| Decide on intuition, because the data is too noisy | No | Weak: gives up the measurable system; the plan wants data as a basis for decisions. |
| Audit first: check what the data really measures, then decide | No | Defends: a decision with an evidence step; slower, but cheap. |

*Trainer note.* The plan asks for a data-driven optimisation strategy with the causes unclear. Strategy 1 is the reference; strategy 4 also defends (audit first); strategy 2 repeats the misreading; strategy 3 gives up data as a basis for decisions. Plan: items 1 to 3.

### Reference: The tracking and analysis rules

**Reference answer.** Track only events that serve a KPI or a hypothesis; Split drop-out by week, device and course; Combine numbers with tests with real learners to learn why; Say what is tracked and get consent where it is required; Write down each KPI's definition and owner

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Track only events that serve a KPI or a hypothesis | Yes | Keeps the data tied to a question and limits the privacy burden. |
| Split drop-out by week, device and course | Yes | Shows where the problem concentrates. |
| Combine numbers with tests with real learners to learn why | Yes | Data shows symptoms; tests show causes. |
| Say what is tracked and get consent where it is required | Yes | Data protection (GDPR) and trust. |
| Write down each KPI's definition and owner | Yes | A KPI nobody owns drifts. |
| Track every click with the learner's name | No | Rejected: beyond need, and a privacy risk. |
| Judge a change after one week of data | No | Rejected: too short to tell a change from noise. |
| Compare this month with last month without checking holidays | No | Rejected: unlike periods. |
| Use average time on page as the only measure of success | No | Rejected: an open tab counts as success. |

*Trainer note.* At least three; any three of the five reference rules defend if each ties tracking to a question, makes the reading fair, or respects the learner. The rejected ones collect beyond need, judge too fast, compare unlike periods or rely on one misleading number. Plan: item 2, analysis and tracking strategy.

### Reference: The hypotheses

**Reference answer.** A hypothesis I can test: items 1, 4 · An assumption to check first: items 2, 6 · A symptom: what the data shows: items 3, 7 · Not a hypothesis: items 5, 8

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| 1 · “We believe the sign-up form has too many fields, so we will cut it to three and expect completion to rise by five points.” | A hypothesis I can test. A change, an expected effect and a figure. |
| 2 · “Learners leave because the content is boring.” | An assumption to check first. A cause that is assumed and has no test yet. |
| 3 · “Drop-out in the first week is 70%.” | A symptom: what the data shows. A figure, not an explanation. |
| 4 · “If we save a learner's quiz answers when they leave, quiz drop-out on phones will fall.” | A hypothesis I can test. A change and an expected effect; open item: it names no figure, which should be added. |
| 5 · “We should improve the user experience.” | Not a hypothesis. No change, no effect, no way to test. |
| 6 · “More marketing will fix it.” | An assumption to check first. An assumed fix with no stated effect or test. |
| 7 · “Time spent per session is short.” | A symptom: what the data shows. A figure, not an explanation. |
| 8 · “The new menu is better.” | Not a hypothesis. Better by what measure? Not testable as stated. |

*Trainer note.* There is no single right grid. Item 4 is the open one (testable, once a figure is added). Items 5 and 8 are not hypotheses. Each bin must be used at least once. A testable hypothesis names a change, an expected effect and, ideally, a figure. Plan: item 3, hypotheses for improvement.

:::note 3.1 · Why this strategy, and not the others?
**Model answer (an example).** I start from hypotheses: the platform already has a lot of data and its problem is how it is read, so more tracking would only add noise. Three KPIs, a written hypothesis for each change and one change at a time turn the data into insight and the insight into a decision, and tracking only what a hypothesis needs also keeps us on the right side of data protection.

**Why.** The reason must rest on insight, the unclear causes and privacy, not on the amount of data (Materi B1).

**Look for.**
- Gives a reason about insight or causes.
- Does not rest on the amount of data.
- Mentions privacy or what is tracked.
:::

:::note 3.1 · Who decides on a change, on what evidence, and what does intuition still decide?
**Model answer (an example).** The head of product decides on a change, with the head of learning, on the first-week drop-out and the result of a test with real learners. Data shows what happened, so what to try first, the wording of a message, and what the platform stands for are still judgement: data is the basis of a decision, not a substitute for thinking.

**Why.** The plan asks for a decision architecture and discusses data against intuition: a role, the evidence, and what data cannot decide (Materi B1, B2).

**Look for.**
- Names a role.
- Names the figure or test.
- Names something intuition still decides.
:::

## Block 3.2 · model answers (UE10)

*Block 3.2: Measures, risk, a decision under uncertainty, and what you give up · Core · about 10 min.*

### Reference: Three prioritised measures

**Reference answer.** D1 A KPI framework · D2 A hypothesis-driven optimisation programme · D3 Privacy-safe tracking and cause analysis. Total €135,000 of €160,000; the longest takes 16 weeks.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D1 · A KPI framework (€30,000, 8 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: without defined KPIs nothing can be judged. Position 1. |
| D2 · A hypothesis-driven optimisation programme (€60,000, 16 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: turns insight into decisions, one change at a time. Position 2. |
| D3 · Privacy-safe tracking and cause analysis (€45,000, 12 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: gives the data a question and keeps it lawful. Position 3. |
| D4 · Qualitative research with learners (€25,000, 10 weeks) | No | Defends in place of D3: data shows symptoms, research shows causes; it does not track change over time. |
| D5 · An AI analytics platform that finds insights automatically (€90,000, 20 weeks) | No | Rejected: patterns without hypotheses repeat the misreading, and the cost is highest. |
| D6 · Track every click and keep all data for good (€40,000, 10 weeks) | No | Rejected: beyond need, a privacy risk, and more noise. |
| D7 · A real-time board dashboard (€35,000, 10 weeks) | No | Rejected: more charts, not more insight. |

*Trainer note.* Reference order: D1, D2, D3 (€135,000 of €160,000, longest 16 weeks). D1 first because without KPIs nothing can be judged. D4, D1, D2 also defends: explain the numbers with research first, since the causes are unclear. D1, D2, D4 defends if the reason names evidence.

### Reference: The biggest risk: misreading the data

**Reference answer.** We misread the data again: a symptom was taken for a cause, and the wrong thing was fixed.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| We misread the data again: a symptom was taken for a cause, and the wrong thing was fixed. | Yes | The misreading pole. Action: no cause is named without a test with real learners; notice it when a fix shows no effect. |
| Data overload: more tracking than anyone could read, and the team stopped looking. | No | Action: three KPIs and tracking only for hypotheses; notice it in dashboards that nobody opens. |
| A privacy failure: tracking went beyond need or notice, and learners or a regulator objected. | No | Action: data protection review and consent before any new tracking; notice it in complaints. |
| We optimised the metric and not the learner: the number rose and learning did not. | No | Action: pair every KPI with an outcome and a test; notice it when a KPI rises and results do not. |
| Analysis paralysis: we waited for perfect data while drop-out kept growing. | No | Action: act on the likely and cheap step with a reversal condition; notice it in the months with no change. |

*Trainer note.* Any of the five defends with a reason and an action. The action should cost less than the risk; ask when the team would notice it. These five are the typical answers of a premortem (Klein 2007). The plan names where data is misinterpreted.

:::note 3.2 · Why does the first measure go first?
**Model answer (an example).** D1 goes first: without defined KPIs nothing can be judged, and the data has no direction. Data leads to insight and insight to a decision: with the KPIs fixed, D2 turns insight into decisions one change at a time, and D3 gives the data a question and keeps it lawful. The three cost €135,000 of the €160,000.

**Why.** The plan's rationale: data → insight → decision. A different first measure defends if the reason says how it turns data into an insight (Materi B1).

**Look for.**
- Says how it turns data into an insight.
- Says why the others can wait.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What do you do about that risk?
**Model answer (an example).** I name no cause until a test with real learners supports it, and every report states the hypothesis beside the number; if a fix shows no effect on the KPI after one cycle of two weeks, I treat the cause as unproven and test the next guess.

**Why.** One action, and a time when the risk would be noticed (Materi B2, B3).

**Look for.**
- One action.
- Says when the risk would be noticed.
:::

:::note 3.2 · One decision you make under uncertainty (without knowing the causes)
**Model answer (an example).** I decide … I decide to define three KPIs (D1) and to run the first hypothesis test on the sign-up form, before I invest in any new tracking. I do not know … I do not know which screen causes most of the drop-out. I reverse if … (a figure) … by … (a time) I reverse if, by the end of month 2, first-week drop-out has not fallen from 70% to below 65% for the learners who see the shorter form, and I test the next hypothesis on the course page.

**Why.** Four parts: what you decide, what you do not know, what would make you reverse it (a figure) and by when, and what you give up (Materi B3).

**Look for.**
- States the decision in one sentence.
- Names a specific unknown.
- Gives a reversal condition with a figure and a time.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What do you give up or postpone?
**Model answer (an example).** I postpone the AI analytics platform (D5) and the real-time board dashboard (D7) this year. The data team will miss the tool they wanted, and the board will not get a live wall of charts.

**Why.** Giving up nothing means you have not decided (Materi B3).

**Look for.**
- Names something real.
- Names who would miss it.
:::
