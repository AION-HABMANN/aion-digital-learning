# Trainer guide

## How to use this document

- **Before the day:** read the lesson plan above and the model solution of the case study (UE5–6). Decide which Optional cards and blocks your group will skip.
- **During the day:** use the section of the unit you are in (UE4 coaching, UE6 debrief, UE7 feedback round, UE10 debrief). Show a model solution only after the participants have worked.
- **Reference answers:** they use the facts and the numbers printed in the Task document. Figures that the plan does not print are Case assumptions. Effort is rated by the printed cost rule taught in Materi A5; user impact and risk are judgements.
- **What is not here:** the Materi (the study material) and the Task (the worksheet) are separate documents and are handed out. This one is not.

## Assessment criteria (Bewertungskriterien)

Use these criteria to read what participants hand in and to give feedback in words. They are the same on every day; the reference answers below say what meets them on this day.

| Criterion | Level | Meets | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The user's side** | 1 | Describes and sorts from the learner's side; every statement points at a printed fact. | Mostly from the learner's side; some statements are about the design only, or no fact is named. | Describes the platform, not the learner; no fact named. |
| **Weighing under limits** | 2 | Rates user impact, effort and risk with a reason for each; effort follows the printed cost rule; budget and time are checked, or the overrun is explained. | Ratings given, reasons thin, or effort does not follow the rule. | Ratings missing or without reasons; the limits are ignored. |
| **Prioritising** | 2 | Gives a reason for position one (reach, cost or what the others depend on); the order fits the ratings. | An order is given; the reason covers the whole set or is generic. | No order, or no reason. |
| **Missing information** | 2 | Names something specific that would change the decision, and how to find it out. | Names a gap but not how to find it out. | “More data” with nothing specific. |
| **Conflict of goals** | 3 | Names the day's conflicting goals, says who or what loses, and justifies the order. | Names the conflict; the loser is not named. | Presents a plan in which everything wins. |
| **Decision under uncertainty** | 3 | Decides in one sentence, names an unknown, gives a reversal figure and a time, and says what is given up. | Decides; the reversal or the give-up is missing or vague. | Does not decide, or gives up nothing. |

**How to give feedback.** Say what is strong, name one criterion that is only partly met, and give one concrete next step. A choice that differs from the reference is not an error if the reason is clear and refers to a printed fact.

# UE2–3 · Level 1 tasks

## Block 1.1 · model answers (UE2)

*Block 1.1: Eight barriers: who do they affect, and which one stops a learner? · Core · about 13 min.*

### Reference: Eight barriers into Visual, Motor, Cognitive

**Reference answer.** Visual: facts 1, 2, 3 · Motor: facts 4, 5 · Cognitive: facts 6, 7, 8

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| 1 · Light grey text, contrast 2.1 : 1 | Visual. Low contrast hits learners with low vision: a visual barrier. |
| 2 · 10 px text that breaks when enlarged | Visual. Small, unscalable text hits learners with low vision: a visual barrier. |
| 3 · A key chart with no text alternative | Visual. A blind learner misses the content entirely: a visual barrier. |
| 4 · A menu that opens only on mouse hover | Motor. A keyboard-only learner cannot open the menu: a motor barrier that stops use completely. |
| 5 · 18 px quiz buttons close together | Motor. Tiny targets hit learners with a tremor or on a phone: a motor barrier. |
| 6 · Seven levels deep, three menus to the same page | Cognitive. Complex navigation overloads learners who find structure hard: a cognitive barrier. |
| 7 · Long paragraphs, abbreviations, no headings | Cognitive. Unstructured text hits learners with dyslexia or limited attention: a cognitive barrier. |
| 8 · A fixed 60-second quiz limit | Cognitive. A fixed limit penalises learners who read or respond more slowly: a cognitive barrier. |

*Trainer note.* The sort is objective in its core: low contrast, small text and a missing text alternative are visual; a mouse-only menu and tiny buttons are motor; deep navigation, unstructured text and a fixed time limit are cognitive. Several barriers affect more than one group (small buttons also matter for low vision; a time limit also matters for motor impairments); the test is whom it hits first. A learner who argues another placement with a reason defends the choice. Which barrier stops a learner completely, the changes and the reasons are judged. Plan: Arbeitsauftrag 1, identify barriers, user groups affected, improvements.

### Reference: The barrier that stops a learner

**Reference answer.** 4 · A menu that opens only on mouse hover

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Light grey text, contrast 2.1 : 1 | No | Slows: the text can be read with effort, or with a zoom or a high-contrast setting. |
| 2 · 10 px text that breaks when enlarged | No | Slows or blocks for low vision; defends if the reason says the page breaks. |
| 3 · A key chart with no text alternative | No | Defends: a blind learner misses the content, though the rest of the page works. |
| 4 · A menu that opens only on mouse hover | Yes | Reference choice: a keyboard-only learner cannot reach any course, so use stops completely. |
| 5 · 18 px quiz buttons close together | No | Slows: errors, but a learner can still try. |
| 6 · Seven levels deep, three menus to one page | No | Slows: findable with effort. |
| 7 · Long paragraphs, abbreviations, no headings | No | Slows: harder to read, not impossible. |
| 8 · A fixed 60-second quiz limit | No | Defends: a learner who needs longer cannot finish the quiz. |

*Trainer note.* Elements 3, 4 and 8 can all stop a learner completely; the others mostly slow. Element 4 is the reference because every course sits behind the menu. Look at the reason: it should say whom it stops and why they cannot go around it. The plan's goal is to raise awareness of barriers.

:::note 1.1 · Whom does it stop, and why is it worse than a barrier that only slows?
**Model answer (an example).** Element 4 stops learners who use only a keyboard, for example because of a motor impairment: the menu opens only on mouse hover and cannot be reached with Tab, so they cannot reach any course. Element 1, low contrast, only slows: the text is hard to read, but a learner can still zoom or change the colours and go on.

**Why.** A barrier that stops leaves the learner no way around it; one that slows can be worked around with effort (Materi A1).

**Look for.**
- Names the user group.
- Says why there is no way around it.
- Names a barrier that only slows.
:::

:::note 1.1 · Which user groups are affected, and what would you change?
**Model answer (an example).** Affected: visual (1 to 3), motor (4, 5) and cognitive (6 to 8). 1. Make the menu open with the keyboard as well as the mouse (barrier 4). 2. Raise the text contrast to at least 4.5 : 1 (barrier 1). 3. Add a text alternative to the chart (barrier 3). 4. Add headings and shorter paragraphs (barrier 7). 5. Let learners extend the quiz time (barrier 8).

**Why.** The plan asks which user groups are affected and which improvements. A good answer names the groups and ties each change to a barrier (Materi A1 to A3).

**Look for.**
- Names the user groups.
- Names the barrier each change answers.
- Starts each change with a verb.
:::

## Block 1.2 · model answers (UE3)

*Block 1.2: Prioritising WCAG measures: three options · Optional · about 8 min.*

### Reference: Three options for WCAG measures

**Reference answer.** Reference order: A (Improve the contrasts), then B (Simplify the navigation), then C (Provide alternative content for all existing items)

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A · Improve the contrasts (€6,000, 2 weeks) | Yes | Cheap and quick, with no risk: one change in the style sheet helps every page at once. Reference ratings: Impact Mid, effort Low (by the printed cost), risk Low; position 1. |
| B · Simplify the navigation (€14,000, 5 weeks) | Yes | A big effect: it removes the keyboard and cognitive barriers behind every course; mid effort. Reference ratings: Impact High, effort Mid (by the printed cost), risk Mid; position 2. |
| C · Provide alternative content for all existing items (€26,000, 9 weeks) | Yes | Necessary for blind and deaf learners but large: many existing items make it costly and slow. Reference ratings: Impact High, effort High (by the printed cost), risk Mid; position 3. |

*Trainer note.* Prioritise practical implementation: a cheap fix that helps every page first.

:::note 1.2 · Justify your prioritisation: prioritise practical implementation
**Model answer (an example).** I put A first: it costs €6,000 and two weeks, nothing can go wrong, and one change helps every page, which suits the time pressure. B comes second: it removes the barriers behind every course and is worth the mid effort. C comes last: it is needed, but with many existing items it costs the most and takes the longest, so I would do the most-used content first and the rest later.

**Why.** The plan's goal is to prioritise practical implementation. A different first choice defends if the reason names the time pressure and the budget.

**Look for.**
- Says what each option frees learners from.
- Names the time pressure or the amount of content.
:::

# UE4 · Coaching: transition from Level 1 to Level 2

## Coaching Focus: Accessibility as a Quality and Compliance Factor (UE4)

**The plan's coaching points.**

- Accessibility is not an add-on, but a standard
- WCAG as a minimum requirement, not as a goal
- Inclusion improves UX for all users
- Strategic importance (law, market, reputation)

**Reflection questions.**

- Who is currently being excluded?
- Which measure has the greatest effect?
- Which risks arise from non-compliance?

**How to run the unit (about 45 minutes).**

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | Say the coaching focus in one sentence: “Accessibility as a Quality and Compliance Factor.” Say that in the next unit they will not describe, they will decide. |
| 2 | 10 | Pairs talk through the reflection questions; the notes go into Block 1.3 (never scored). |
| 3 | 15 | Collect two or three answers. Link each to a fact or a finding from Block 1.1, so that the answer is about this platform and not about UX in general. |
| 4 | 10 | Bridge: Level 1 was reading the platform from the user's side. Level 2 asks you to rate and order measures under a budget and a deadline, and to say what information you are missing. Show the first step of Block 2.2. |
| 5 | 5 | Open questions; set the briefing for UE5. |

# UE5–6 · Case study (2 UE)

## The plan's model solution: core logic (UE5–6)

Show this only after the participants have worked, at the end of UE6.

- **Main problem:** lack of accessibility
- **Measures (as the plan lists them):**
  - Improve contrast
  - Simplify navigation
  - provide alternative content
  - clear structure

**Rationale (plan):** Accessibility = usability = success

## Block 2.1 · model answers (UE5–6)

*Block 2.1: Four central barriers to the platform · Optional · about 7 min.*

### Reference: Four central barriers

**Reference answer.** WCAG is not implemented: there are no rules for contrast, text alternatives or keyboard use.; There is no inclusive UX: accessibility was not considered when the screens were designed.; The barriers hit several user groups at once: visual, motor and cognitive.; Nobody tests the platform with a keyboard, a screen reader or users with impairments.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| WCAG is not implemented: there are no rules for contrast, text alternatives or keyboard use. | Yes | Facts 1, 3 and 4 and the case: a missing WCAG implementation. |
| There is no inclusive UX: accessibility was not considered when the screens were designed. | Yes | Facts 4 to 8 and the case: no inclusive UX. |
| The barriers hit several user groups at once: visual, motor and cognitive. | Yes | Facts 1 to 8 cover all three groups. |
| Nobody tests the platform with a keyboard, a screen reader or users with impairments. | Yes | Facts 3 and 4: barriers that a simple test would have shown. |
| Learners with impairments are not skilled enough with technology. | No | It blames the learner; the facts show barriers in the platform. |
| The platform's colours need a new brand design. | No | A look matter; contrast is only one fact of eight. |
| Users with impairments should be given a separate simplified version. | No | It is a remedy, not a problem, and it contradicts inclusive design. |

*Trainer note.* A problem is defended by a printed fact. The plan's analysis names a missing WCAG implementation and no inclusive UX; the Musterlösung names the lack of accessibility as the main problem. The rejected options include the tempting “a separate simplified version”, which is a remedy that contradicts inclusive design; none of them explains why users with impairments cannot use the platform.

:::note 2.1 · Which printed fact supports your first problem?
**Model answer (an example).** Fact 4: the course menu cannot be reached with the Tab key, which is a missing WCAG requirement and a barrier a keyboard test would have found.

**Why.** A problem is defended by a printed fact. The plan's Musterlösung names the lack of accessibility as the main problem.

**Look for.**
- Points at a fact by number or quotes the case.
- Says why it explains the barrier.
:::

# UE7 · Feedback round

## Feedback with a Strategic Perspective (UE7)

**Preparation (from the plan).**

- Presentation of the solutions

**Level 3 input: ask these questions.**

- Which measure has the greatest reach?
- Where is accessibility underestimated?
- Which risks arise from non-implementation?

**Discussion.**

- Accessibility vs. costs
- UX as social responsibility

**How to run the unit (about 45 minutes).**

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | Each group presents for about five minutes. The focus is the reasoning, not the result: why this first priority, what was left out. |
| 2 | 15 | Put the Level 3 input questions to the whole room, one at a time. Ask for the answer in business terms: what it moves (completion, retention, engagement, cost). |
| 3 | 10 | Run the discussion. Close with one sentence: the same measures, ordered under a different goal, give a different plan. That is the step to Level 3. |

## Transition from Level 2 to Level 3 (Übergang)

Level 2 asks a participant to decide among measures. Level 3 asks them to decide as a manager, between goals that pull apart. The day's conflict is: **Conflicting goals: accessibility against cost, standardisation against flexibility** (Materi B2).

**A participant is ready for Level 3 (Route 2, Task 2) when:**

1. the Core of Task 1 is done and the reason for position one names a reach, a cost or a dependency;
2. the missing information is specific and comes with a way to find it out;
3. they can say the day's conflict in one sentence and name what loses when each side wins.

**If a participant is not there yet:**

- Ask for the reason of position one again, using the “How to decide” box of Materi A5.
- Pair them for UE8 with someone who is ready, for the brief and the first block.
- Give them the “In plain words” box of Materi B1 before the brief; Route 2 does not need Route 1 answers.

Route 2 does not require Route 1: a participant may start the transfer project without having finished Task 1, and use their own answers in the memo if they have them.

## Block 3.1 · model answers (UE9)

*Block 3.1: The WCAG strategy, the process and the evaluation · Core · about 10 min.*

### Reference: The strategy for WCAG

**Reference answer.** Step by step: WCAG 2.2 level AA on the most-used courses first, then build it into the process

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Step by step: WCAG 2.2 level AA on the most-used courses first, then build it into the process | Yes | Reference strategy: starts where most learners are, and stops new barriers from appearing. |
| Everything at once: all 2,000 items in one programme | No | Weak: over the budget, slow, and learners wait for any gain. |
| Only the legal minimum, fixed when a complaint arrives | No | Weak: reactive; treats WCAG as the goal, not the minimum; the risk stays. |
| Audit first: test the whole platform, then decide | No | Defends: a decision with an evidence step; slower, but cheap. |

*Trainer note.* The plan asks for a strategy for implementing WCAG within a limited budget. Strategy 1 is the reference; strategy 4 also defends (audit first); strategy 3 is the weakest because it treats WCAG as the goal and leaves the risk. Plan: item 1.

### Reference: The process steps

**Reference answer.** Accessibility criteria in every design brief; A check at every design review; A keyboard and screen-reader test before every release; Training for designers and content authors; Accessible components in the style guide

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Accessibility criteria in every design brief | Yes | Early: the barrier is stopped before it is designed. |
| A check at every design review | Yes | A quality gate that does not depend on one person. |
| A keyboard and screen-reader test before every release | Yes | Catches what automated tools miss. |
| Training for designers and content authors | Yes | People who make the content know the rules. |
| Accessible components in the style guide | Yes | Every new page starts from accessible parts. |
| A test only before the launch of a big change | No | Rejected: too late and too rare. |
| An overlay plug-in as the fix | No | Rejected: does not remove the barriers underneath. |
| A separate team that fixes problems afterwards | No | Rejected: late, costly, and teams keep creating new barriers. |
| Automated tests only | No | Rejected: automated tools find only a part of the barriers. |

*Trainer note.* At least three; any three of the five reference steps defend if each stops barriers early and does not depend on one person. The rejected ones are late, narrow or do not remove the barrier. Plan: item 3, integration into UX processes.

### Reference: The evaluation indicators

**Reference answer.** Measure automatically: items 1, 2 · Test with users: items 3, 4 · Report to management: items 5, 6 · Does not show accessibility: items 7, 8

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| 1 · The share of pages that pass the contrast check | Measure automatically. A tool can count it. |
| 2 · The share of images with a text alternative | Measure automatically. A tool can count it; whether the text is useful needs a person. |
| 3 · Can a keyboard-only user finish a lesson? | Test with users. Only a person trying it can tell. |
| 4 · How long a screen-reader user needs to find lesson 3 | Test with users. A task test with a real user. |
| 5 · Accessibility complaints per month | Report to management. A figure management reads; open item: it also shows where to test. |
| 6 · Open barriers by severity, reported each quarter | Report to management. The progress view for management. |
| 7 · The number of pages on the platform | Does not show accessibility. Says nothing about accessibility. |
| 8 · A published statement that the platform “is accessible” | Does not show accessibility. A claim, not a measurement. |

*Trainer note.* There is no single right grid. Item 5 is the open one (report or test). Items 7 and 8 do not show accessibility. Each bin must be used at least once. A good approach mixes automatic counts, tests with users and a figure for management. Plan: item 4, evaluation approach (measurability).

:::note 3.1 · Why this strategy, and not the others?
**Model answer (an example).** I implement WCAG step by step, starting with the most-used courses: the budget is limited, so fixing everything at once is not possible, and the most-used courses reach the most learners soonest. I then build accessibility into the process so that new content does not bring new barriers, because WCAG is the minimum and not the goal.

**Why.** The reason must rest on reach, cost and risk, and on WCAG as a minimum, not only on what the law demands (Materi B1).

**Look for.**
- Gives a reason about reach, cost or risk.
- Treats WCAG as a minimum.
- Says what comes after the first step.
:::

:::note 3.1 · Which measure has the greatest reach, and who else gains?
**Model answer (an example).** A keyboard-operable menu and clear structure reach the most learners: keyboard-only and screen-reader users, and learners who find structure hard. Everyone else gains too: a learner on a phone, in a hurry, or with a broken mouse also finds courses faster, which shows that inclusion improves UX for all.

**Why.** The plan's Level 3 input asks for the measure with the greatest reach; inclusion improves UX for all (Materi B1).

**Look for.**
- Names the measure.
- Names the groups it reaches.
- Names a group beyond people with impairments.
:::

## Block 3.2 · model answers (UE10)

*Block 3.2: Measures, risk, a decision under uncertainty, and what you give up · Core · about 10 min.*

### Reference: Three prioritised measures

**Reference answer.** D1 WCAG 2.2 AA on the most-used courses · D2 Accessibility built into the UX process · D3 Measurement: automated checks and quarterly user tests. Total €140,000 of €180,000; the longest takes 20 weeks.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D1 · WCAG 2.2 AA on the most-used courses (€70,000, 20 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: removes the barriers where most learners meet them. Position 1. |
| D2 · Accessibility built into the UX process (€40,000, 12 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: stops new barriers from appearing, cheaply, early. Position 2. |
| D3 · Measurement: automated checks and quarterly user tests (€30,000, 10 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: makes progress visible to management. Position 3. |
| D4 · Training for designers and authors (€20,000, 8 weeks) | No | Defends in place of D3: builds skills so fewer barriers are made, though progress is harder to show. |
| D5 · An accessibility overlay (€15,000, 4 weeks) | No | Rejected: does not remove the barriers underneath, and gives a false sense of safety. |
| D6 · A separate accessible version (€90,000, 24 weeks) | No | Rejected: separate is not inclusive, doubles the upkeep, and the cost is the highest. |
| D7 · Fix only what complaints report (€25,000, 12 weeks) | No | Rejected: reactive; the risk stays, and learners who do not complain stay excluded. |

*Trainer note.* Reference order: D1, D2, D3 (€140,000 of €180,000, longest 20 weeks). D1 first because it removes existing barriers where most learners meet them. D2, D1, D3 also defends: process first, so that new content stays accessible while old content is fixed. D1, D2, D4 defends if the reason names skills.

### Reference: The biggest risk: costs against benefits

**Reference answer.** Non-compliance: we did too little, and a complaint led to legal action and lost customers.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Non-compliance: we did too little, and a complaint led to legal action and lost customers. | Yes | The non-implementation pole. Action: fix the most-used courses first and publish a dated plan; notice it in the complaint count. |
| Cost overrun: retrofitting the old content cost far more than planned. | No | Action: fix the most-used content first and set a cap; notice it in cost per item after the first month. |
| A one-off project: new content brought new barriers, and the gains faded. | No | Action: build checks into the process; notice it in the share of new pages that fail. |
| We fixed what automated tools found, but real users still could not use the platform. | No | Action: test with real users each quarter; notice it in task results. |
| The team lacked the skills, and the fixes were wrong. | No | Action: train designers and authors; notice it in failed checks. |

*Trainer note.* Any of the five defends with a reason and an action. The action should cost less than the risk; ask when the team would notice it. These five are the typical answers of a premortem (Klein 2007). The plan's Level 3 input asks which risks arise from non-implementation.

:::note 3.2 · Why does the first measure go first?
**Model answer (an example).** D1 goes first: it removes the existing barriers where most learners meet them, which also lowers the legal risk soonest. With the first courses fixed, D2 stops new barriers from appearing, and D3 shows management the progress. Accessibility is usability is success, so the order goes from fixing to preventing to measuring. The three cost €140,000 of the €180,000.

**Why.** The plan's rationale: accessibility = usability = success. A different first measure defends if the reason names whom it reaches and what it prevents (Materi B1).

**Look for.**
- Says whom it reaches.
- Says what it prevents or why the others can wait.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What do you do about that risk?
**Model answer (an example).** I fix the most-used courses first and publish a dated plan for the rest, and I count accessibility complaints each month; if they do not fall after the first three months, I move budget to the barrier they name most.

**Why.** One action, and a time when the risk would be noticed (Materi B2, B3).

**Look for.**
- One action.
- Says when the risk would be noticed.
:::

:::note 3.2 · One decision you make under uncertainty (without knowing how much of the platform fails WCAG)
**Model answer (an example).** I decide … I decide to start with WCAG 2.2 AA on the twenty most-used courses (D1) and to build checks into the design review (D2), before I audit the rest. I do not know … I do not know how many barriers the other courses have. I reverse if … (a figure) … by … (a time) I reverse if, by the end of month 4, the audit of ten further courses shows more than five serious barriers per course, and I widen the first step to the audit's worst courses.

**Why.** Four parts: what you decide, what you do not know, what would make you reverse it (a figure) and by when, and what you give up (Materi B3).

**Look for.**
- States the decision in one sentence.
- Names a specific unknown.
- Gives a reversal condition with a figure and a time.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What do you give up or postpone?
**Model answer (an example).** I postpone the accessibility of the rarely used courses and I do not buy an overlay this year. The authors of the old courses will miss a quick fix, and sales cannot yet claim that every course is accessible.

**Why.** Giving up nothing means you have not decided (Materi B3).

**Look for.**
- Names something real.
- Names who would miss it.
:::
