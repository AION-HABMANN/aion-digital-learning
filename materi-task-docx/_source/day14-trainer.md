# Trainer guide

## How to use this document

- **Before the day:** read the lesson plan above and the model solution of the case study (UE5–6). Decide which Optional cards and blocks your group will skip.
- **During the day:** use the section of the unit you are in (UE4 coaching, UE6 debrief, UE7 feedback round, UE10 debrief). Show a model solution only after the participants have worked.
- **Reference answers:** they use the facts and the numbers printed in the Task document. Figures that the plan does not print are Case assumptions. Effort is rated by the printed cost rule taught in Materi A5; user impact and risk are judgements.
- **What is not here:** the Materi (the study material) and the Task (the worksheet) are separate documents and are handed out. This one is not.

## Assessment criteria (Bewertungskriterien)

Use these criteria to read what participants hand in and to give feedback in words. They are the same on every day; the reference answers below say what meets them on this day.

| Criterion | Level | Meets | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The user's side** | 1 | Describes and sorts from the learner's side; every statement points at a printed fact. | Mostly from the learner's side; some statements are about the design only, or no fact is named. | Describes the platform, not the learner; no fact named. |
| **Weighing under limits** | 2 | Rates user impact, effort and risk with a reason for each; effort follows the printed cost rule; budget and time are checked, or the overrun is explained. | Ratings given, reasons thin, or effort does not follow the rule. | Ratings missing or without reasons; the limits are ignored. |
| **Prioritising** | 2 | Gives a reason for position one (reach, cost or what the others depend on); the order fits the ratings. | An order is given; the reason covers the whole set or is generic. | No order, or no reason. |
| **Missing information** | 2 | Names something specific that would change the decision, and how to find it out. | Names a gap but not how to find it out. | “More data” with nothing specific. |
| **Conflict of goals** | 3 | Names the day's conflicting goals, says who or what loses, and justifies the order. | Names the conflict; the loser is not named. | Presents a plan in which everything wins. |
| **Decision under uncertainty** | 3 | Decides in one sentence, names an unknown, gives a reversal figure and a time, and says what is given up. | Decides; the reversal or the give-up is missing or vague. | Does not decide, or gives up nothing. |

**How to give feedback.** Say what is strong, name one criterion that is only partly met, and give one concrete next step. A choice that differs from the reference is not an error if the reason is clear and refers to a printed fact.

# UE2–3 · Level 1 tasks

## Block 1.1 · model answers (UE2)

*Block 1.1: Eight hurdles: which kind, which one first, and a plainer text · Core · about 13 min.*

### Reference: Eight hurdles into Language, Structure, Usability

**Reference answer.** Language: facts 1, 2, 3 · Structure: facts 4, 5 · Usability: facts 6, 7, 8

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| 1 · A 52-word welcome sentence | Language. A very long sentence is hard to follow: a language hurdle. |
| 2 · Unexplained terms: “asynchronous modality” | Language. Jargon without an explanation keeps newcomers out: a language hurdle. |
| 3 · An error message: “Error 4012: invalid payload” | Language. A technical message says nothing to a learner: a language hurdle. |
| 4 · Eleven menu entries, labels “Hub” and “Resources 2” | Structure. Too many entries with vague names: a structure hurdle. |
| 5 · One course in three places with different content | Structure. The same thing in several places confuses: a structure hurdle. |
| 6 · An assignment can be sent with no file | Usability. A preventable mistake is allowed: an error-prevention hurdle. |
| 7 · One action named “Save”, “Submit” and “Apply” | Usability. The same action under different names: a consistency hurdle. |
| 8 · Five clicks to mark a lesson done | Usability. A common action takes too many steps: an efficiency hurdle. |

*Trainer note.* The sort is objective in its core: a long sentence, jargon and a cryptic error message are language; a crowded menu with vague labels and the same course in three places are structure; a preventable mistake, one action under three names and five clicks for a common task are usability (error prevention, consistency, efficiency). Fact 3 could also be argued as error prevention; the test is whether the words alone are the problem. A learner who argues another placement with a reason defends the choice. Which hurdle makes learners give up first, the plainer text and the reasons are judged. Plan: Arbeitsauftrag 1, comprehension hurdles, which users, a more understandable version.

### Reference: The hurdle that comes first

**Reference answer.** 1 · A 52-word welcome sentence

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · A 52-word welcome sentence | Yes | Reference choice: it is the first thing every learner reads, and nobody can act on what they do not understand. |
| 2 · Unexplained terms | No | Defends: it is on the same first screen and keeps newcomers out. |
| 3 · An error message “Error 4012” | No | Defends later: it appears only when something goes wrong. |
| 4 · Eleven menu entries | No | Defends: if the learner cannot find the course, they stop. |
| 5 · One course in three places | No | Slows after the first minute. |
| 6 · An assignment with no file | No | Later: reached only after learning has started. |
| 7 · One action under three names | No | Later: noticed only across several pages. |
| 8 · Five clicks to finish a lesson | No | Later: reached only after a lesson. |

*Trainer note.* Elements 1, 2 and 4 all defend as the first hurdle; 3 and 5 to 8 come later in a learner's path. Element 1 is the reference because every learner reads the welcome text first. Look at the reason: it should say why this comes before the others, for a new learner. The plan's goal is to develop an awareness of language.

:::note 1.1 · Why does it come first, and for which users is it most problematic?
**Model answer (an example).** The 52-word welcome sentence comes first because every learner reads it before anything else, and a learner who does not understand the first screen has no reason to go on. It is most problematic for new learners, non-native speakers and learners with dyslexia, who need short sentences and everyday words.

**Why.** The reason must say why the hurdle comes before the others in a learner's path, and name the user groups it hurts (Materi A1, A2).

**Look for.**
- Says why it comes before the others.
- Names the user groups.
:::

:::note 1.1 · Write a more understandable version of the welcome text
**Model answer (an example).** Welcome to ClearLearn. Here you learn at your own pace, in short lessons. Start with “My first lesson”. You can stop at any time, and we save where you were.

**Why.** The plan asks for a more understandable version. A good answer has short sentences, everyday words, no jargon and a clear first step (Materi A1).

**Look for.**
- Short sentences.
- Everyday words and no jargon.
- A clear first step for the learner.
:::

## Block 1.2 · model answers (UE3)

*Block 1.2: Prioritising UX simplification: three options · Optional · about 8 min.*

### Reference: Three options for simplification

**Reference answer.** Reference order: A (Simplify the content), then B (Improve the navigation), then C (Add additional functions)

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A · Simplify the content (€9,000, 3 weeks) | Yes | It goes to the cause: users do not understand; moderate cost and quick. Reference ratings: Impact High, effort Mid (by the printed cost), risk Low; position 1. |
| B · Improve the navigation (€14,000, 5 weeks) | Yes | It helps learners find things; useful, but it does not make the content understandable. Reference ratings: Impact Mid, effort Mid (by the printed cost), risk Mid; position 2. |
| C · Add additional functions (€32,000, 8 weeks) | Yes | It adds complexity to a platform that is already too complex; high effort and risk. Reference ratings: Impact Low, effort High (by the printed cost), risk High; position 3. |

*Trainer note.* Weigh comprehensibility against functionality: complexity is UX's greatest enemy.

:::note 1.2 · Justify your decision: weigh comprehensibility against functionality
**Model answer (an example).** I put A first: users do not understand the content, so simplifying it goes to the cause, it costs €9,000 and takes three weeks, which suits the time pressure. B comes second: finding things is part of the problem, but a clear menu does not help if the content is still hard. C comes last: more functions add complexity to a platform that is already too complex, and cost the most.

**Why.** The plan's goal is to weigh comprehensibility against functionality. A different first choice defends if the reason names the cause and the time pressure.

**Look for.**
- Says what each option does for understanding.
- Names the cause or the time pressure.
:::

# UE4 · Coaching: transition from Level 1 to Level 2

## Coaching Focus: Comprehensibility Is a Strategic UX Quality (UE4)

**The plan's coaching points.**

- Complexity is UX's greatest enemy
- Simple systems are more successful
- Usability determines usage
- Testing as a continuous process

**Reflection questions.**

- Where do users not understand the system?
- Which simplification has the greatest effect?
- Which complexity is necessary?

**How to run the unit (about 45 minutes).**

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | Say the coaching focus in one sentence: “Comprehensibility Is a Strategic UX Quality.” Say that in the next unit they will not describe, they will decide. |
| 2 | 10 | Pairs talk through the reflection questions; the notes go into Block 1.3 (never scored). |
| 3 | 15 | Collect two or three answers. Link each to a fact or a finding from Block 1.1, so that the answer is about this platform and not about UX in general. |
| 4 | 10 | Bridge: Level 1 was reading the platform from the user's side. Level 2 asks you to rate and order measures under a budget and a deadline, and to say what information you are missing. Show the first step of Block 2.2. |
| 5 | 5 | Open questions; set the briefing for UE5. |

# UE7 · Feedback round

## Feedback with a Strategic Perspective (UE7)

**Preparation (from the plan).**

- Presentation of the solutions

**Level 3 input: ask these questions.**

- Which measure improves understanding the most?
- Where is complexity underestimated?
- Which decision has the greatest influence on usage?

**Discussion.**

- Simplicity vs. functionality
- UX as a comprehensibility problem

**How to run the unit (about 45 minutes).**

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | Each group presents for about five minutes. The focus is the reasoning, not the result: why this first priority, what was left out. |
| 2 | 15 | Put the Level 3 input questions to the whole room, one at a time. Ask for the answer in business terms: what it moves (completion, retention, engagement, cost). |
| 3 | 10 | Run the discussion. Close with one sentence: the same measures, ordered under a different goal, give a different plan. That is the step to Level 3. |

## Transition from Level 2 to Level 3 (Übergang)

Level 2 asks a participant to decide among measures. Level 3 asks them to decide as a manager, between goals that pull apart. The day's conflict is: **Conflicting goals: simplicity against information, standard against flexibility** (Materi B2).

**A participant is ready for Level 3 (Route 2, Task 2) when:**

1. the Core of Task 1 is done and the reason for position one names a reach, a cost or a dependency;
2. the missing information is specific and comes with a way to find it out;
3. they can say the day's conflict in one sentence and name what loses when each side wins.

**If a participant is not there yet:**

- Ask for the reason of position one again, using the “How to decide” box of Materi A5.
- Pair them for UE8 with someone who is ready, for the brief and the first block.
- Give them the “In plain words” box of Materi B1 before the brief; Route 2 does not need Route 1 answers.

Route 2 does not require Route 1: a participant may start the transfer project without having finished Task 1, and use their own answers in the memo if they have them.

## Block 3.1 · model answers (UE9)

*Block 3.1: The simplification strategy, the information architecture and the testing concept · Core · about 10 min.*

### Reference: The strategy for simplifying

**Reference answer.** In steps: plain language and a clear structure first, then test and adjust

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| In steps: plain language and a clear structure first, then test and adjust | Yes | Reference strategy: goes to the first hurdles, and learns from users before the next step. |
| Simplify everything to the minimum at once | No | Weak: risks removing what some learners need, with no test. |
| Keep the platform as it is and add a help centre | No | Weak: explains the complexity instead of reducing it. |
| Test first: find where learners stop, then decide | No | Defends: a decision with an evidence step; slower, but cheap. |

*Trainer note.* The plan asks for a strategy for simplifying content. Strategy 1 is the reference; strategy 4 also defends (test first); strategy 2 risks over-simplification; strategy 3 is the weakest because it leaves the complexity. Plan: item 1.

### Reference: The information architecture steps

**Reference answer.** One menu with at most six entries; Each course in exactly one place; A path above each page (“you are here”); A “continue where you stopped” card on the home page; The same label for the same action everywhere

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One menu with at most six entries | Yes | Fewer choices; less to scan. |
| Each course in exactly one place | Yes | One place for each thing. |
| A path above each page (“you are here”) | Yes | Orientation. |
| A “continue where you stopped” card on the home page | Yes | Efficiency for the most common return. |
| The same label for the same action everywhere | Yes | Consistency. |
| A mega menu that lists every course | No | Rejected: brings back too many choices. |
| Three menus that lead to the same page | No | Rejected: the same thing in several places. |
| Labels in internal words such as “Hub” | No | Rejected: jargon in the navigation. |
| A search box as the only way to find things | No | Rejected: needs the right word, which newcomers do not know. |

*Trainer note.* At least three; any three of the five reference steps defend if each gives a thing one place and one name, or shows where the learner is. The rejected ones add choices, duplicate places or use jargon. Plan: item 2, optimisation of the information architecture.

### Reference: The testing and optimisation activities

**Reference answer.** Before the change: items 1 · During the test: items 2, 3 · After the change: items 4, 5, 6 · Does not show understanding: items 7, 8

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| 1 · A card sort of the menu labels with learners | Before the change. Finds the labels before they are built. |
| 2 · Test the first draft of a rewritten text with five learners | During the test. Tests the draft with real readers. |
| 3 · Ask learners to explain a screen back in their own words | During the test. A comprehension check. |
| 4 · Compare first-week drop-out before and after the change | After the change. A data-based check of the effect. |
| 5 · Track support questions about the same screen each month | After the change. Continuous evaluation. |
| 6 · An A/B test of two menu versions | After the change. Open item: during the rollout also defends; compares two versions with real use. |
| 7 · The number of page views per month | Does not show understanding. Shows use, not understanding. |
| 8 · The team's opinion of whether the text sounds good | Does not show understanding. The team knows the platform; not a test of understanding. |

*Trainer note.* There is no single right grid. Item 6 is the open one (during the rollout or after). Items 7 and 8 do not show understanding. Each bin must be used at least once. A good concept tests before, during and after, and repeats. Plan: item 3, testing and optimisation concept.

:::note 3.1 · Why this strategy, and not the others?
**Model answer (an example).** I simplify in steps: the first hurdles are the language and the structure, and both are cheap to fix, so I start there. I then test with learners and adjust, because simplifying everything at once could remove what some learners need, and a help centre would only explain the complexity instead of reducing it.

**Why.** The reason must rest on comprehensibility and usage and name the risk of simplifying too much (Materi B1, B2).

**Look for.**
- Gives a reason about comprehensibility or usage.
- Names the risk of too much simplification.
- Says what comes after the first step.
:::

:::note 3.1 · Which complexity is necessary, and which would you remove?
**Model answer (an example).** I keep the depth of the expert courses: the subject is demanding, and cutting it would take away what advanced learners need. I remove the complexity around it: eleven menu entries, jargon on the first screens and three names for one action, because they add effort that teaches nothing.

**Why.** The plan's conflict is simplicity against information content: separate the complexity of the subject from the complexity of the interface (Materi B2).

**Look for.**
- Names a necessary complexity.
- Names an unnecessary one.
- Gives the reason.
:::

## Block 3.2 · model answers (UE10)

*Block 3.2: Measures, risk, a decision under uncertainty, and what you give up · Core · about 10 min.*

### Reference: Three prioritised measures

**Reference answer.** D1 A plain-language programme · D2 A new information architecture · D3 A continuous testing and optimisation loop. Total €130,000 of €170,000; the longest takes 14 weeks.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D1 · A plain-language programme (€50,000, 14 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: goes to the first hurdle and is the cheapest cure. Position 1. |
| D2 · A new information architecture (€45,000, 12 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: gives each thing one place and one name. Position 2. |
| D3 · A continuous testing and optimisation loop (€35,000, 10 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: testing as a continuous process; it shows whether the others worked. Position 3. |
| D4 · Training authors in plain language (€18,000, 8 weeks) | No | Defends in place of D3: builds skills so new text stays plain, though progress is harder to show. |
| D5 · New functions: chat, forum and calendar (€80,000, 20 weeks) | No | Rejected: adds complexity, and the cost is highest. |
| D6 · A help centre and a manual (€30,000, 10 weeks) | No | Rejected: explains the complexity instead of reducing it. |
| D7 · Cut half of the content (€10,000, 4 weeks) | No | Rejected: removes necessary content, the over-simplification risk. |

*Trainer note.* Reference order: D1, D2, D3 (€130,000 of €170,000, longest 14 weeks). D1 first because comprehensibility leads to usage and success. D3, D1, D2 also defends: test first, since nobody knows where learners stop. D1, D2, D4 defends if the reason names skills.

### Reference: The biggest risk: too much against too little simplification

**Reference answer.** Too much simplification: we removed information that some learners needed, and experts left.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Too much simplification: we removed information that some learners needed, and experts left. | Yes | The over-simplification pole. Action: separate the subject's complexity from the interface's and test with experts too; notice it in complaints from advanced learners. |
| Too little simplification: learners still did not understand, and drop-out stayed high. | No | The under-simplification pole. Action: test understanding after each step; notice it in first-week drop-out. |
| An uneven rollout: some screens became clear while others stayed as they were, so the platform felt inconsistent. | No | Action: a style guide and one team that owns the words; notice it in the share of screens that follow it. |
| We tested opinion, not understanding, so we did not see that learners still got lost. | No | Action: use task tests and comprehension checks; notice it when ratings rise and drop-out does not. |
| The platform grew complicated again within a year, because nobody kept the rules. | No | — |

*Trainer note.* Any of the five defends with a reason and an action. The action should cost less than the risk; ask when the team would notice it. These five are the typical answers of a premortem (Klein 2007). The plan names too much against too little simplification.

:::note 3.2 · Why does the first measure go first?
**Model answer (an example).** D1 goes first: the first hurdle is language, and plain language is the cheapest cure and reaches every learner. Comprehensibility leads to usage and usage to success: with the words fixed, D2 gives each thing one place and one name, and D3 shows with tests and data whether it worked and what to fix next. The three cost €130,000 of the €170,000.

**Why.** The plan's rationale: comprehensibility → usage → success. A different first measure defends if the reason names what it does for understanding (Materi B1).

**Look for.**
- Says what it does for understanding.
- Says why the others can wait.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What do you do about that risk?
**Model answer (an example).** I keep the expert courses untouched and simplify only the interface around them, and I ask advanced learners to test every change; if their complaints rise in the first two months, I roll the change back.

**Why.** One action, and a time when the risk would be noticed (Materi B2, B3).

**Look for.**
- One action.
- Says when the risk would be noticed.
:::

:::note 3.2 · One decision you make under uncertainty (without knowing where learners stop)
**Model answer (an example).** I decide … I decide to start with plain language on the thirty most-used screens (D1) and a first test with eight learners, before I rebuild the menu. I do not know … I do not know on which screen most learners stop. I reverse if … (a figure) … by … (a time) I reverse if, by the end of month 3, first-week drop-out has not fallen from 40% to below 35%, and I look at the structure before I rewrite more text.

**Why.** Four parts: what you decide, what you do not know, what would make you reverse it (a figure) and by when, and what you give up (Materi B3).

**Look for.**
- States the decision in one sentence.
- Names a specific unknown.
- Gives a reversal condition with a figure and a time.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What do you give up or postpone?
**Model answer (an example).** I postpone the new chat, forum and calendar (D5) and I do not cut any content this year. The community team will miss the functions they planned, and some editors will miss the chance to remove old lessons.

**Why.** Giving up nothing means you have not decided (Materi B3).

**Look for.**
- Names something real.
- Names who would miss it.
:::
