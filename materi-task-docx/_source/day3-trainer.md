# Trainer guide

## How to use this document

- **Before the day:** read the lesson plan above and the model solution of the case study (UE5–6). Decide which Optional cards and blocks your group will skip.
- **During the day:** use the section of the unit you are in (UE4 coaching, UE6 debrief, UE7 feedback round, UE10 debrief). Show a model solution only after the participants have worked.
- **Reference answers:** they use the facts and the numbers printed in the Task document. Figures that the plan does not print are Case assumptions. Effort is rated by the printed cost rule taught in Materi A5; user impact and risk are judgements.
- **What is not here:** the Materi (the study material) and the Task (the worksheet) are separate documents and are handed out. This one is not.

## Assessment criteria (Bewertungskriterien)

Use these criteria to read what participants hand in and to give feedback in words. They are the same on every day; the reference answers below say what meets them on this day.

| Criterion | Level | Meets | Partly | Not yet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The user's side** | 1 | Describes and sorts from the learner's side; every statement points at a printed fact. | Mostly from the learner's side; some statements are about the design only, or no fact is named. | Describes the platform, not the learner; no fact named. |
| **Weighing under limits** | 2 | Rates user impact, effort and risk with a reason for each; effort follows the printed cost rule; budget and time are checked, or the overrun is explained. | Ratings given, reasons thin, or effort does not follow the rule. | Ratings missing or without reasons; the limits are ignored. |
| **Prioritising** | 2 | Gives a reason for position one (reach, cost or what the others depend on); the order fits the ratings. | An order is given; the reason covers the whole set or is generic. | No order, or no reason. |
| **Missing information** | 2 | Names something specific that would change the decision, and how to find it out. | Names a gap but not how to find it out. | “More data” with nothing specific. |
| **Conflict of goals** | 3 | Names the day's conflicting goals, says who or what loses, and justifies the order. | Names the conflict; the loser is not named. | Presents a plan in which everything wins. |
| **Decision under uncertainty** | 3 | Decides in one sentence, names an unknown, gives a reversal figure and a time, and says what is given up. | Decides; the reversal or the give-up is missing or vague. | Does not decide, or gives up nothing. |

**How to give feedback.** Say what is strong, name one criterion that is only partly met, and give one concrete next step. A choice that differs from the reference is not an error if the reason is clear and refers to a printed fact.

# UE2–3 · Level 1 tasks

## Block 1.1 · model answers (UE2)

*Block 1.1: Eight facts: amount, form, or order and purpose? · Core · about 13 min.*

### Reference: Eight facts into Amount, Form, Order and purpose

**Reference answer.** Amount: fact 1, fact 2, fact 3 · Form: fact 4, fact 5, fact 6 · Order and purpose: fact 7, fact 8

| Item | Reference reading |
|---|---|
| Fact 1 · One continuous text of about 500 words | Amount. The learner has to take in 500 words at once: amount. |
| Fact 2 · Banner, chat, recommendations and a nine-entry menu next to the text | Amount. Several things compete for attention at once: amount. |
| Fact 3 · All 12 quiz questions on one page | Amount. Twelve questions at once is a question of how much is shown together: amount. |
| Fact 4 · No paragraphs, headings or lists | Form. The content is not shaped into parts the eye can use: form. |
| Fact 5 · No picture, and terms not explained | Form. The presentation offers neither a picture nor an explanation: form. |
| Fact 6 · The key sentence looks like every other sentence | Form. Nothing marks what matters most, so attention has nothing to follow: form (visual hierarchy). |
| Fact 7 · 28 lessons in one flat list | Order and purpose. The learner cannot see groups or an order: order and purpose. |
| Fact 8 · No lesson states its goal or its link to the one before | Order and purpose. The learner cannot tell what a lesson is for or how it fits: order and purpose. |

*Trainer note.* The sort is objective: each fact belongs to one of the three observations of the case. Two edges: fact 5 (no picture, terms not explained) can read as amount of knowledge needed, but what the page offers to explain is a matter of how the content is shaped, so it is Form; fact 3 (12 quiz questions on one page) is Amount even though it is a quiz layout. The one fact that makes a learner stop first and how the process feels are judged: any of the eight defends if the reason is written from the learner's side (intake, processing, storage). Plan: Arbeitsauftrag 1, describe 5 problems from the user's side.

:::note 1.1 · The fact that would make a learner stop first, and how the process feels
**Model answer (an example).** I open Lesson 7 and see one block of about 500 words. I would not know where to start, and by the fifth sentence I would have lost track of the first, so I would close the page.

**Why.** Any of the eight facts defends. What the plan asks for is the user's side (“Beschreiben Sie, wie sich der Lernprozess anfühlt”): the answer must say what the learner cannot do or hold in mind (intake, processing, storage), not that something looks wrong.

**Look for.**
- Written from the learner's side (“I would not know…”, “I would lose track…”).
- Names a consequence: a step of learning that fails (what to look at, what to hold, what to keep).
- Refers to the fact chosen.
:::

:::note 1.1 · What you would improve intuitively
**Model answer (an example).** I would split the lesson into short parts with headings (facts 1 and 4), take the banner, chat and extra menu off the lesson page (fact 2), and state the goal of each lesson and group the 28 lessons in order (facts 7 and 8).

**Why.** The plan asks what the learner would improve intuitively. A good answer names a change and the fact it answers, so the change is traceable to the evidence.

**Look for.**
- Names at least two changes, each tied to a fact by its number.
- The changes act on the load (amount, form, order), not on the look.
:::

## Block 1.2 · model answers (UE3)

*Block 1.2: Reduce the load of a module: three options · Optional · about 8 min.*

### Reference: Reduce the load of a module: three options

**Reference answer.** Greatest effect: Split the content into small modules. Order: C, B, A. Effort by the printed cost: A Low, B High, C Mid.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A · Shorten the content significantly (€7,500, 2 weeks) | No | Last: the content is professionally necessary and the learners are beginners, so shortening a lot removes what they need. Effort Low, impact Low, risk High. |
| B · Visualise the content (diagrams, graphics) (€22,000, 4 weeks) | No | Second: a diagram helps where a structure or flow is described, not everywhere; effort High by the rule, and it takes the whole four weeks. Impact Mid, risk Mid. |
| C · Split the content into small modules (€10,000, 3 weeks) | Yes | Reference: chunking reduces what the learner holds at once without deleting content. Impact High, effort Mid by the rule, risk Low. |

*Trainer note.* The plan's rationale for the day: learning capacity > amount of information.

:::note 1.2 (Optional) · Which option has the greatest effect on learning
**Model answer (an example).** Splitting the content into small modules has the greatest effect: the learner has less to hold at once and nothing they need is removed. Shortening removes content that beginners came for.

**Why.** The plan's rationale: learning capacity before the amount of information. A good answer refers to what the learner has to hold, find or understand.

**Look for.**
- Refers to what the learner holds, finds or understands.
- Notes whether needed content is kept.
:::

# UE4 · Coaching: transition from Level 1 to Level 2

## Coaching Focus: UX = Steering Attention & Thinking (UE4)

**The plan's coaching points.**

- UX decides whether learning takes place at all
- Good UX does not reduce thinking – it guides it
- Difference: "simple" vs. "effective for learning"
- How do you recognize cognitive overload?
- Prioritization: What must be understood – and what not?

**Reflection questions.**

- Where do we overburden users unnecessarily?
- Which information is truly decisive?
- Which UX decision measurably improves learning?

**How to run the unit (about 45 minutes).**

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | Say the coaching focus in one sentence: “UX = Steering Attention & Thinking.” Say that in the next unit they will not describe, they will decide. |
| 2 | 10 | Pairs talk through the reflection questions; the notes go into Block 1.3 (never scored). |
| 3 | 15 | Collect two or three answers. Link each to a fact or a finding from Block 1.1, so that the answer is about this platform and not about UX in general. |
| 4 | 10 | Bridge: Level 1 was reading the platform from the user's side. Level 2 asks you to rate and order measures under a budget and a deadline, and to say what information you are missing. Show the first step of Block 2.2. |
| 5 | 5 | Open questions; set the briefing for UE5. |

## Block 1.3 · model answers (UE4)

:::note 1.3 (Optional) · Coaching reflection
**Model answer (an example).** On the lesson page, where a banner, a chat and a nine-entry menu compete with the text. The key sentence of each lesson and the goal of the lesson: they tell the learner what to hold on to. Splitting lessons into short units, because a learner can then explain what a unit said, which is measurable.

**Why.** Reflection is never scored. The plan's coaching points: UX steers attention and thinking; good UX does not reduce thinking but guides it; “simple” is not the same as “effective for learning”. Use the learners' notes as the start of the live discussion.
:::

# UE5–6 · Case study (2 UE)

## The plan's model solution: core logic (UE5–6)

Show this only after the participants have worked, at the end of UE6.

- **Main problem:** extraneous cognitive load
- **Measures (as the plan lists them):**
  - Chunking (splitting up)
  - Visualization
  - clear structure
  - Reduction of irrelevant content

**Rationale (plan):** Learning capacity > amount of information

## Block 2.1 · model answers (UE5–6)

*Block 2.1: Four causes of cognitive overload · Optional · about 7 min.*

### Reference: Four causes of cognitive overload

**Reference answer.** Too much information is shown at once. · The content has no visual structure. · The learner cannot see a learning logic: groups, order, goals. · Technical terms are used without explanation.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Too much information is shown at once. | Yes | The case names it, and facts 1 to 3 show it. |
| The content has no visual structure. | Yes | The case names it, and facts 4 to 6 show it. |
| The learner cannot see a learning logic: groups, order, goals. | Yes | The case names it (“no recognisable learning logic”), and facts 7 and 8 show it. |
| Technical terms are used without explanation. | Yes | The case says the text has unexplained technical terms, and fact 5 shows it. |
| The subject itself is complex. | No | True, and it is the intrinsic load, but it is not a cause of overload that design can remove; design can only order it. |
| The visual style of the platform looks dated. | No | No printed fact says so; it is about the surface, not the load. |
| The course catalogue is too small. | No | Nothing in the case says learners are overloaded for lack of courses. |

*Trainer note.* The plan's model solution names the main problem as extraneous cognitive load. “The subject itself is complex” is true and is the intrinsic load, but it is not a cause that design can remove; a learner who ticks it and says so defends the choice, and the key marks it as not expected only because the task asks for causes the design can act on.

:::note 2.1 (Optional) · Which printed fact supports your first cause
**Model answer (an example).** The case itself names too much information at once, no visual structure and no recognisable learning logic, and facts 1 to 8 show each one; fact 5 shows the unexplained technical terms.

**Why.** A cause is defended by a printed fact. The plan's model solution names the main problem as extraneous cognitive load.

**Look for.**
- Points at a printed fact (by its number) or quotes a case line.
- Does not offer a cause the case never mentions.
:::

## Block 2.2 · model answers (UE5–6)

*Block 2.2: Four improvements, rated, ordered and justified · Core · about 17 min.*

### Reference: Choose four measures, rate them, order them

**Reference answer.** Chunking; Clear structure; Remove extras from the lesson page; Goal and outline. Total €30,000 of €40,000, longest 3 weeks of 4.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| M1 · Chunking | Yes | Answers a printed fact (amount). Effort Mid by the printed cost €10,000. Reference ratings: impact High, risk Low. |
| M2 · Visualisation | No | In the plan's own list, and defendable: visualisation helps where a flow or structure is described. At €20,000 and 5 weeks it is High effort and one week over the four-week limit, so the reference set uses “Goal and outline” instead. |
| M3 · Clear structure | Yes | Answers a printed fact (form). Effort Low by the printed cost €7,000. Reference ratings: impact Mid, risk Low. |
| M4 · Remove extras from the lesson page | Yes | Answers a printed fact (amount). Effort Low by the printed cost €4,000. Reference ratings: impact Mid, risk Mid. |
| M5 · Goal and outline | Yes | Answers a printed fact (order). Effort Mid by the printed cost €9,000. Reference ratings: impact Mid, risk Low. |
| M6 · Points for lesson views | No | Rejected: points for lesson views reward opening a lesson, not understanding it, and add one more thing to the screen. |
| M7 · New brand look | No | Rejected: it changes the look, not the load; no printed fact is about the look. |
| M8 · An AI chatbot | No | Rejected: €48,000 and 10 weeks is over both limits, and a chatbot adds one more thing to the screen. |
| M9 · Extra reading library | No | Rejected: more reading material adds load; learners are already overloaded by the amount. |

*Trainer note.* Visualisation (M2) takes 5 weeks against the 4-week limit; the reference set uses M5 instead. The plan's rationale: learning capacity > amount of information.

:::note 2.2 · Why the first priority goes first
**Model answer (an example).** Chunking goes first because it removes the biggest load, the 500-word block, for every learner without deleting content. Learning capacity matters more than the amount of information.

**Why.** The plan's rationale: learning capacity > amount of information. A different first priority defends if the reason is concrete.

**Look for.**
- Gives a reason for position one, not for the whole set.
- Refers to the learner's problem, the cost or what the others depend on.
:::

:::note 2.2 · What information you are missing
**Model answer (an example).** I do not know whether learners are overloaded by the amount of content or by the wording of the technical terms. I would watch five beginners read Lesson 7 and ask each to explain the key point.

**Why.** The plan asks it outright (“Welche Information fehlt Ihnen?”). A good answer names a specific unknown that would change the decision, and a way to find it out.

**Look for.**
- Names a specific unknown, not “more data”.
- Says how it could be found out.
- Connects to the decision.
:::

## Debrief of the case study (UE6)

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15 | Groups of three or four compare their four measures and their order. They name where they differ and why. |
| 2 | 15 | Each group gives the reason for its first priority in one sentence. Write the reasons on a board; do not write the measures. |
| 3 | 10 | Show the plan's core logic and the rationale line (“Learning capacity > amount of information”). Ask: where does a different order still defend? |
| 4 | 5 | Ask each participant to write one thing they would now rate differently. |

# UE7 · Feedback round

## Feedback with a Learning-Impact Perspective (UE7)

**Preparation (from the plan).**

- Presentation of the solutions

**Level 3 input: ask these questions.**

- Which measure improves learning the most?
- Which decision reduces the drop-out rate?
- Where is the risk of "too much simplification"?

**Discussion.**

- Efficiency vs. deep learning
- UX as a learning amplifier

**How to run the unit (about 45 minutes).**

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | Each group presents for about five minutes. The focus is the reasoning, not the result: why this first priority, what was left out. |
| 2 | 15 | Put the Level 3 input questions to the whole room, one at a time. Ask for the answer in business terms: what it moves (completion, retention, engagement, cost). |
| 3 | 10 | Run the discussion. Close with one sentence: the same measures, ordered under a different goal, give a different plan. That is the step to Level 3. |

## Transition from Level 2 to Level 3 (Übergang)

Level 2 asks a participant to decide among measures. Level 3 asks them to decide as a manager, between goals that pull apart. The day's conflict is: **Conflicting goals: efficiency, deep learning and over-simplification** (Materi B2).

**A participant is ready for Level 3 (Route 2, Task 2) when:**

1. the Core of Task 1 is done and the reason for position one names a reach, a cost or a dependency;
2. the missing information is specific and comes with a way to find it out;
3. they can say the day's conflict in one sentence and name what loses when each side wins.

**If a participant is not there yet:**

- Ask for the reason of position one again, using the “How to decide” box of Materi A5.
- Pair them for UE8 with someone who is ready, for the brief and the first block.
- Give them the “In plain words” box of Materi B1 before the brief; Route 2 does not need Route 1 answers.

Route 2 does not require Route 1: a participant may start the transfer project without having finished Task 1, and use their own answers in the memo if they have them.

## Block 3.1 · model answers (UE9)

*Block 3.1: Strategy, definition and three measures · Core · about 9 min.*

### Reference: Three measures for the strategy

**Reference answer.** Rebuild the ten most-used lessons; A content standard with editorial review; A learner-testing routine for new content

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D1 · Rebuild the ten most-used lessons (€60,000, 20 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: it removes extraneous load for the most learners (Materi B1: structure and chunking first) and keeps the content. |
| D2 · A content standard with editorial review (€25,000, 12 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: a content standard is the decision logic made concrete; every new lesson passes the same checks, so the problem does not return. |
| D3 · A learner-testing routine for new content (€30,000, 52 weeks) | Yes | Reference pick: it is the evidence the case lacks (the plan asks to decide without user data), and it shows both effectiveness and efficiency. |
| D4 · Cut the specialist content by a third (€15,000, 8 weeks) | No | Rejected in the plan's own logic: the content is professionally necessary, so cutting a third is the over-simplification risk (Materi B2). |
| D5 · Result and next step after every lesson (€30,000, 12 weeks) | No | Defendable: feedback after every lesson helps storage and gives a measure of what learners can do; it is a candidate for next year. |
| D6 · An AI summary button (€70,000, 24 weeks) | No | Rejected: €70,000 and 24 weeks for a tool that shortens lessons on demand, without checking whether the shortened version still teaches. |
| D7 · A new visual brand (€45,000, 20 weeks) | No | Rejected: it changes the look, not the load; no printed fact is about the look. |

*Trainer note.* A different set of three defends if the reason is written and the three together answer the definition of learning-effective UX.

:::note 3.1 · Your strategy to reduce cognitive load
**Model answer (an example).** I will first reduce what does not help learning, clutter and unstructured text, in the lessons most learners open, and I will protect the professional depth of the content.

**Why.** Materi A2 and B1: cut extraneous load first, keep the intrinsic difficulty the course exists to teach. A strategy that only reduces has not weighed the conflict.

**Look for.**
- Names what is reduced first.
- Names what is protected.
- One to three sentences.
:::

:::note 3.1 · Your definition of learning-effective UX
**Model answer (an example).** A learning-effective UX is one in which a beginner can reach the stated learning goal of a lesson with as little unnecessary effort as possible, shown by being able to explain the key point in their own words afterwards.

**Why.** Materi B1: the definition names the learner, the goal, the cost to be kept low and the proof. A definition without a proof cannot be tested.

**Look for.**
- Names the learner (intended level).
- Names the goal.
- Names the cost kept low (effort that does not help learning).
- Names the proof (explain or do).

**Typical mistakes.**
- “Easy to use” with no proof of learning.
- A satisfaction rating as the only proof.
:::

:::note 3.1 · Why the first measure goes first
**Model answer (an example).** Rebuilding the ten most-used lessons goes first: it removes extraneous load for the most learners, keeps the content and shows a visible result, so effectiveness and efficiency both rise there.

**Why.** The roadmap is an order with a reason. The model order is rebuild, standard, testing routine; a different order defends if the reason is concrete.

**Look for.**
- Gives a reason for position one.
- Says what it does for learning effectiveness and cognitive efficiency.
:::

## Block 3.2 · model answers (UE10)

*Block 3.2: Risk, decision logic, a decision under uncertainty, and what you give up · Core · about 11 min.*

### Reference: The biggest risk

**Reference answer.** We measure completion but not what learners can do.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The content is now so light that learners finish but cannot apply it. | No | Defendable as the biggest risk if the learner says what they do about it; the model picks the measurement risk because the plan's question is where the risk of too much simplification lies. |
| The content is still too complex, and learners still leave. | No | Same reading as the row above. |
| We measure completion but not what learners can do. | Yes | The one that hides both other risks: without a measure of what learners can do, “too simple” and “too complex” cannot be told apart (Materi B2). The remedy is a check at the end of each lesson. |
| The budget is used up before a first result is visible. | No | Defendable as the biggest risk if the learner says what they do about it; the model picks the measurement risk because the plan's question is where the risk of too much simplification lies. |
| Authors ignore the new content standard. | No | Same reading as the row above. |

*Trainer note.* Any of the five defends when the plan to meet it is concrete (what is done, by whom, when). The wrong answer is a risk with no action.

### Reference: Who decides, and which checks a new lesson passes

**Reference answer.** Who: The head of learning with the content lead. Checks: One stated learning goal; A size that fits the learner's time; The key sentence is marked; Technical terms are explained in place; A short check or task at the end.

| Option | In the reference answer? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides: The Chief Learning Experience Officer alone | No | Defendable, but one person's taste is exactly what a decision logic should prevent. |
| Who decides: The head of learning with the content lead | Yes | Learning judgement and content ownership together: neither one person's taste nor the author's own view. |
| Who decides: The author of the lesson | No | Rejected: an author checking their own lesson has no outside view. |
| Who decides: A steering group with management | No | Defendable for large budget items; too slow for every lesson. |
| Check: One stated learning goal | Yes | A check that can be done on every lesson and that follows from the three loads. |
| Check: A size that fits the learner's time | Yes | Same reading as the row above. |
| Check: The key sentence is marked | Yes | Same reading as the row above. |
| Check: Technical terms are explained in place | Yes | Same reading as the row above. |
| Check: A short check or task at the end | Yes | Same reading as the row above. |
| Check: A test with beginners before release | No | Defendable and strong, but not on every lesson: five beginners per module before release is the testing routine (D3), not a per-lesson check. |
| Check: Approval by the most senior person in the room | No | Rejected: authority is not a check on whether a lesson teaches. |

*Trainer note.* At least three checks are required; which ones is judged on the reason in the memo, not on matching the model. A style guide that nobody checks is not a decision logic (Materi B3).

:::note 3.2 · What you do about the biggest risk
**Model answer (an example).** I add a short check at the end of each rebuilt lesson, so that we measure what learners can do next to completion.

**Why.** A risk with no action is only a worry. The action should be concrete: what is done, and when it is noticed. To tell too simple from too complex apart you need a completion measure and a measure of what learners can do.

**Look for.**
- Names an action.
- Says when or how the risk would be noticed.
:::

:::note 3.2 · The decision without user data
**Model answer (an example).** I decide to rebuild one course in small modules and test it with five beginners. I do not know whether learners are overloaded by the amount or by the wording. I will reverse it if fewer than four of five beginners can explain the key point after a lesson.

**Why.** The frame of Materi B3: what I decide, what I do not know, what would make me reverse and by when. A checkable reversal condition has a figure and a time.

**Look for.**
- States the decision.
- States what is not known.
- Gives a checkable reversal condition with a figure and a time.

**Typical mistakes.**
- A reversal condition no one could check (“if it does not work”).
- A decision that waits for data instead of choosing a step that can be undone.
:::

:::note 3.2 · What you give up or postpone
**Model answer (an example).** I give up the full diagram redesign and the AI summary button this year; learners keep the plain text versions of the less-used lessons for now.

**Why.** The skill of Level 3: a decision that gives up nothing has not decided. The answer names something real that a stakeholder would miss.

**Look for.**
- Names a concrete thing given up or postponed.
- It is something someone would miss, not a throwaway.
:::

## Debrief of the transfer project (UE10)

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 25 | Each participant presents the decision under uncertainty in the four parts: what I decide, what I do not know, when I reverse (a figure and a time), what I give up. Two minutes each; the others ask one question. |
| 2 | 10 | Name the losers: what did each plan give up, and who would miss it? A plan with no loser has not decided. |
| 3 | 10 | Show the model solution of Block 3.1 and 3.2 and the plan's goals: think like a decision-maker, understand the day as a system, take responsibility. Compare, do not correct. |
